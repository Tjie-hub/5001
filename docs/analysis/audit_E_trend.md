## I. Core Concepts and Market Structure

### The Six Tenets of Dow Theory
The Dow Theory, derived from the editorials of Charles H. Dow and later organized by William Peter Hamilton, Robert Rhea, and E. George Schaefer, serves as the precursor to modern technical analysis. It is built upon six foundational principles:

1.  **The Market Has Three Movements:**
    *   **Primary Movement:** The major trend (bullish or bearish) lasting from less than a year to several years.
    *   **Medium Swing:** Also known as a secondary reaction or intermediate reaction. It lasts from ten days to three months and typically retraces 33% to 66% of the primary price change.
    *   **Short Swing:** Minor movements or "market noise" lasting from hours to a month.
2.  **Market Trends Have Three Phases:**
    *   **Accumulation Phase:** Informed investors buy or sell against general market opinion. Price remains relatively stable as these investors absorb the supply or demand.
    *   **Public Participation Phase:** Trend followers and technical investors enter the market as it "catches on," leading to rapid price changes and rampant speculation.
    *   **Distribution Phase:** Informed investors begin to exit the market, distributing their holdings to the public.
3.  **The Stock Market Discounts All News:** Stock prices quickly incorporate all available information, a premise that aligns with the efficient-market hypothesis.
4.  **Stock Market Averages Must Confirm Each Other:** Historically, a bull market in industrial companies was not considered valid unless the railway average (now the Transportation Average) also rallied. Divergence between these averages is viewed as a warning of an impending trend change.
5.  **Trends Are Confirmed by Volume:** Price movements on high volume represent the "true" market view and indicate a developing trend. Low-volume movements may be misleading.
6.  **Trends Exist Until Definitive Signals Prove They Have Ended:** Trends should be given the benefit of the doubt despite temporary reversals. Distinguishing between a secondary reaction and a new trend is a primary challenge for theorists.

### Trend Following Strategies
Trend following is a systematic strategy based on the expectation that price movements will continue in their current direction.

*   **Philosophy:** Traders do not forecast or predict specific price levels; they "jump on" a trend after it is established and "ride it" until it exhausts.
*   **Key Indicators:** Common tools for identifying direction include simple moving averages (SMA), current market price calculations, and channel breakouts.
*   **Behavioral Drivers:** Trends are often fueled by herding behavior, confirmation bias (investors seeking info that confirms their beliefs), and risk-management models that trigger buying in up markets and selling in down markets.
*   **Risk Management:** This strategy emphasizes cutting losses early and "letting profits run." Position sizing is determined by the size of the trading account and the volatility of the asset.
*   **Market Preference:** Trend following is often more effective in commodities, which tend to trend strongly, compared to stocks, which may be mean-reverting.

### Swing Trading Methodologies
Swing trading is a speculative strategy focused on profiting from price "swings" over a period of one or more days.

*   **Timeframe:** Positions are held longer than day trades but shorter than long-term buy-and-hold strategies.
*   **Objective Rules:** To eliminate subjectivity and emotion, swing traders often use mathematically based objective rules for entry and exit.
*   **Examples of Systems:**
    *   **Alexander Elder’s Strategy:** Uses three different moving averages. Long trades are only entered when all three are aligned upward; short trades only when they are aligned downward.
    *   **Momentum Signals:** Analysts often use 52-week highs/lows as signals for buy and sell recommendations.
*   **Primary Challenge:** Identifying precise entry and exit points. Unlike trend following, swing trading often seeks to capture gains within price oscillations rather than long-term trends.

## II. Short-Answer Practice Questions

**1. How does Dow Theory define the relationship between the Industrial and Transportation averages?**
Dow Theory posits that the two averages must confirm each other. For a trend (such as a bull market) to be valid, both averages must move in the same direction. If they diverge, it is considered a warning that the trend may be changing.

**2. What are the three elements used in trend-following risk management?**
Risk management in trend following involves the number of shares or futures held (position size), the current market price, and the current market volatility.

**3. Distinguish between the accumulation and distribution phases in market trends.**
The accumulation phase occurs when astute investors buy assets against the general market opinion while prices are stable. The distribution phase occurs at the end of a trend when those same investors sell their holdings to the general public during a period of rampant speculation.

**4. What is a "secondary reaction" in the context of Dow Theory?**
A secondary reaction (or medium swing) is a movement that runs contrary to the primary trend. It typically lasts from ten days to three months and retraces between one-third and two-thirds of the previous primary price move.

**5. Why might a trend-following strategy be less correlated among different practitioners?**
Correlation is often low because different trend followers employ different techniques, calculations, and timeframes to identify and signal the general direction of the market.

**6. What is the primary purpose of using objective rules in swing trading?**
Objective rules are used to eliminate subjectivity, emotional interference, and the labor-intensive nature of manual analysis.

## IV. Glossary of Important Terms

*   **Accumulation Phase:** The initial stage of a trend where "informed" investors begin buying or selling against the majority view.
*   **Confirmation:** The requirement in Dow Theory that multiple market averages (e.g., Industrials and Transportation) must move in the same direction to validate a trend.
*   **Distribution Phase:** The final stage of a major trend where informed investors sell their holdings to a speculative public.
*   **Don't Fight the Tape:** A trading maxim advising investors not to trade against the current market trend.
*   **Drawdown:** The peak-to-trough decline during a specific period for an investment or trading account.
*   **Mean Reverting:** The tendency of an asset's price to move back toward an average over time; a characteristic often attributed to stocks rather than commodities.
*   **Primary Movement:** The longest-term trend in Dow Theory, potentially lasting for several years.
*   **Secondary Reaction:** A medium-term price movement (10 days to 3 months) that moves in the opposite direction of the primary trend.
*   **Swing Trading:** A speculative strategy where assets are held for days to weeks to profit from price oscillations.
*   **Trend Following:** A systematic strategy that seeks to profit from the persistence of market momentum by buying rising assets and selling falling ones.
*   **Volume:** The number of shares or contracts traded in a security, used in Dow Theory to confirm the strength and validity of a price trend.
